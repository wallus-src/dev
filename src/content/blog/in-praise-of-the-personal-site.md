Every few years, someone publishes an elegy for blogging — the feeds won, the algorithm flattened everything, nobody reads personal sites anymore. And every few years, the people who kept their sites quietly get the last word: their URLs still resolve, their archives still grow, and the platform that replaced them has since been replaced itself.

The personal site is not a nostalgia act. It is the only form of publishing on the web where you own the floor under your feet.

## The feed rents; the site owns

On a platform, your writing is inventory — fuel for a timeline, measured in engagement, evictable without notice. The relationship is landlord and tenant, and the lease renews daily at the landlord's pleasure.

A personal site inverts every term:

- **The URL is permanent.** Not because permanence is easy, but because it's *yours* to maintain. Links that worked in 2004 still work — an achievement no platform can match.
- **The design is yours.** Ugly or beautiful, slow or fast — the page looks like what you made, not like the container your words were poured into.
- **The archive compounds.** A feed scatters your work into reverse-chronological mist. A site accumulates it into something with a shape — a body of work.

> A personal site is the difference between keeping a garden and renting a plot in someone else's mall. Both grow things. Only one is yours when the lease changes.

## The craft argument

There is a practical benefit too, the one nobody advertises: a personal site is the best full-stack tutorial ever devised. To keep one you will learn, in this order, DNS, TLS, static hosting, typography, accessibility, RSS, build pipelines, and — somewhere around year two — the humbling art of CSS. Every one of those skills transfers.

It is also a permission-free portfolio. You never have to ask whether a thought is *relevant* enough to post. The bar for publishing on your own site is exactly as high as you set it — which turns out to be the best editorial training there is.

## Start ugly, keep forever

The mistake most people make is treating the site as a project to finish. It is not a project; it is a practice — closer to a garden than to a building. The right way to start is embarrassingly small: one page, one essay, a URL you own. The rest accretes.

Twenty years from now your posts will not be measured against anyone's feed. They will be measured by whether they still exist, still load, still say what you meant. On that timeline, the personal site isn't just the best option on the web.

It is the only one that was ever really yours.
